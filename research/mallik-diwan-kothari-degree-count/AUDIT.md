# Source and review

## Statement and prior work

Amit Kumar Mallik, Ajit A. Diwan and Nishad Kothari, *Extremal minimal bipartite matching covered graphs*, Journal of Combinatorics 17 (2026), 559–606, [DOI 10.4310/joc.260511231633](https://doi.org/10.4310/joc.260511231633), [arXiv:2404.06445v3, §8](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.06445v3#S8). The target is Conjecture 8.9, the main conjecture in §8.1. It relates the number of degree-$k+1$ vertices to both the order and the number of edges. It has no large-order restriction.

- The source's Theorem 8.25 gives equality examples from isomorphic leaf matching on trees. Its star case uses the two complete bipartite blocks and a low-to-low perfect matching. Its Lemma 8.20 guarantees only $t-k$-extendability for this unaugmented block graph when $k\lt t\lt 2k$: indeed the whole high block fails Plummer's $k$-extendability test. The counterexample adds precisely the missing $2k-t$ high-to-high edges as a matching. Each is then indispensable. The original equality construction is credited to the proposers.
- As the source notes, the lower bound in Conjecture 8.10 follows from 8.9, but the converse was not stated. All our examples satisfy 8.10, which asks for at least $n/2+2$ vertices of degree $k+1$.
- Xiaoling He's [arXiv:2611.29504v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.29504v1) (22 November 2026) proves the **eventual edge bound** in Conjecture 8.11 for $k\geq3$, with threshold $n\geq6k$. This neither proves nor disproves the degree-count inequality in 8.9. His §4 examples for sharpness of the edge threshold are different: the complete blocks have smaller part size $s=\lfloor(n-2k)/4\rfloor\leq k-1$, and the cyclic matching graph between the larger parts is regular of degree $k-s+1\geq2$. Our construction uses complete blocks with smaller part size $k$, a degree-one matching between the larger parts, and essential edges joining the smaller parts. In He's examples, putting $N=n/2$, at least $2(N-s)$ vertices have degree $k+1$. With $m=(k+s+1)(N-s)$, the deficit $m-n+2k-(2k-1)(N-s)$ equals $(s-k+2)(N-s)-2N+2k$. It is nonpositive: if $s\leq k-2$ the first term is nonpositive; if $s=k-1$ the expression is $k+1-N\leq0$. Thus his edge-threshold counterexamples do not themselves refute 8.9.
- Lou's earlier bounds, quoted as Corollaries 8.6–8.8 in the source, have different constants and do not settle 8.9. The $k=2$ brace results of [Fabres, Kothari and Carvalho](https://doi.org/10.1002/jgt.22628) concern 2-extendability; no assertion about the remaining $k=2$ instance of 8.9 is made here.

## Mathematical cross-check

A finite edge-deletion search first produced the row masks in [verify.py](verify.py). Grouping the vertices by degree reveals the two complete blocks and the two matchings. The combinatorial proof applies to the entire two-parameter range and does not rely on the search.

Two separate arguments check the graph property: Plummer's neighborhood test, and direct construction of a perfect matching after arbitrary $k$ deletions on each side, with explicit nonextending matchings after any edge deletion. The checker independently implements the original definition on the literal 16-vertex graph by exhaustively considering edge triples and using exact matching recursion. All 3,420 three-edge matchings extend, and each of the 36 edge deletions admits a nonextending three-edge matching. A separate subset-union routine checks neighborhood expansion and all edge deletions on all 15 family members for $3\leq k\leq7$. Internal cross-check only; no external referee or Lean verification is claimed.
