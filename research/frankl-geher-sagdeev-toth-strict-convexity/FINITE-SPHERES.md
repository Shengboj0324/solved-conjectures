# Frankl–Gehér–Sagdeev–Tóth’s strictly convex coloring conjecture

The authors ask for a countable coloring in which all isometric copies of a given infinite subset of a finite-dimensional strictly convex normed space contain every color. They prove it for strictly convex planar $`\ell_p`$ norms and in some higher-dimensional cases with a bisector hypothesis. We obtain all strictly convex normed planes from a transfinite coloring theorem on arbitrary metric spaces. The conjecture in unrestricted dimension remains open here. [Primary statement following Theorem 2.1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00454-024-00702-5) · [Research issue](https://github.com/1337isnot1337/solved-conjectures/issues/856)

**Finite-sphere theorem.** Let $`X`$ be a metric space and suppose that, for some fixed $`k\geq1`$, the intersection of any $`k`$ spheres with distinct centers and specified radii is finite. For every infinite metric space $`M`$ there is a coloring $`X\to\mathbb N`$ such that each isometric copy of $`M`$ in $`X`$ contains infinitely many points of every color. It suffices to use a fixed countably infinite sequence of distinct points of $`M`$, with distance matrix $`(d_{ij})`$; below an edge is the image of an isometric embedding of that sequence.

For a $`k`$-element set $`A\subset X`$, the union of edges containing $`A`$ is countable. Indeed there are countably many assignments of distinct sequence indices to $`A`$. For each assignment, location of index $`j`$ outside the anchors belongs to a finite intersection of the prescribed $`k`$ spheres. The union over the countably many indices and assignments is countable.

We prove by induction on $`|Y|`$ for every $`Y\subset X`$ that $`Y`$ can be colored so every edge $`e`$ with $`e\setminus Y`$ finite has infinitely many points of each color in $`e\cap Y`$.

For $`Y`$ countable, fix $`k`$ distinct anchor points in $`Y`$, their indices, and an integer $`r\geq0`$. For each nonanchor index take its finite discrete set of possible locations in $`X`$ given the anchor distances. The product of these sets is compact; an empty coordinate simply means that no such embedding exists. The equations for all pairwise distances are closed, since any condition on finitely many discrete coordinates is closed. The requirement that at most $`r`$ coordinates are outside $`Y`$ is also closed, since any violation is witnessed by $`r+1`$ coordinates. We obtain a compact space of embeddings, all with infinite traces on $`Y`$. For each finite set $`D\subset Y`$, the open sets “some coordinate equals $`y`$,” $`y\in Y\setminus D`$, cover this compact space. A finite subcover therefore gives a finite subset of $`Y\setminus D`$ hitting every one of these embeddings.

There are countably many anchor choices, index assignments and $`r`$. At stage $`m`$, choose a fresh finite subset of $`Y`$ hitting all of the first $`m`$ compact families, using their finite union and taking $`D`$ to be the previously colored points. Color it with the $`m`$th entry of a sequence of naturals in which every color recurs infinitely often; color leftover vertices arbitrarily. Any edge almost contained in $`Y`$ has at least $`k`$ anchors there and is covered by one of these families. This proves the countable step.

For uncountable $`\kappa=|Y|`$, call $`Z\subset Y`$ closed if every edge with $`k`$ points in $`Z`$ has all its points of $`Y`$ in $`Z`$. Closing any subset of size $`\lambda`$ costs at most $`\max(\lambda,\aleph_0)`$: add the countable unions attached to each $`k`$-tuple, and iterate countably often. Unions of nested closed sets are closed. Well-order $`Y`$ in type $`\kappa`$ and let $`Z_\alpha`$ be the closure in $`Y`$ of its first $`\alpha+1`$ points, for $`\alpha<\kappa`$. These are nested, have size below $`\kappa`$ even when $`\kappa`$ is singular, and cover $`Y`$. Color each disjoint layer $`Z_\alpha\setminus\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}Z_\beta`$ by the smaller-cardinal induction hypothesis. For an edge almost contained in $`Y`$, choose the first stage containing $`k`$ of its points in $`Y`$. This exists by finiteness of $`k`$. It contains its whole trace, while the union of preceding stages has at most $`k-1`$ of the edge’s vertices. Thus the edge is almost contained in this single layer and receives all colors infinitely often. This completes the induction.

## The geometry of a strictly convex normed plane

Two norm circles of positive radii and different centers meet in at most two points. For completeness consider the two homothetic strictly convex compact disks. For equal radii align their relative translation horizontally. The endpoints of a horizontal section of one disk are functions $`l(y),u(y)`$ on its vertical projection, strictly convex and strictly concave respectively. A common boundary point requires $`u(y)-l(y)`$ equal to the positive translation distance and is then unique in that section. Strict concavity gives at most two such levels.

For different radii, put the homothety center at the origin and write the disks as $`B,tB`$ with $`t>1`$. If $`0\in B`$, strict convexity gives no common boundary point except possibly $`0`$. Otherwise strict separation allows coordinates with $`x>0`$ on $`B`$. The projectivity $`(x,y)\mapsto(y/x,1/x)`$ sends $`B`$ to a strictly convex compact disk whose vertical sections have endpoints $`0<l(s)\leq u(s)`$. It sends $`tB`$ to the same sections scaled vertically by $`1/t`$. A common boundary point requires $`u(s)-t l(s)=0`$ and is unique at such a section. The strict concavity of this function gives at most two. At extreme section parameters the section is a singleton and cannot meet its distinct positive rescaling. Zero radii are immediate.

The finite-sphere theorem with $`k=2`$ therefore applies to every strictly convex normed plane, with no smoothness, algebraicity or bisector-complexity hypothesis. It works for bounded and unbounded configurations alike.
